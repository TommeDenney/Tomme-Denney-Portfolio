# AR Work of Art

**A 360° mixed-reality rhythm game where the artwork is not what you look at — it is what your playing builds.**

*Solo · Immersive Media: XR History & Theory (XR 5010), Northeastern · 2024 · Unity, Meta Quest 3 · Downloadable on itch.io*

## The idea

Most virtual art asks you to look. The question I wanted to answer was whether an audience could be given the pen instead — not *interact with* a piece, but be the reason it exists in the state you find it in.

So the piece is a rhythm game. Blocks arrive around you in a full sphere, aligned to wherever you happen to be facing. Hitting them fires particle effects into the space; missing them makes those effects fade. Three audio tracks layer in one after another as you sustain performance. The artwork accumulates while you play well and erodes while you do not.

## Where it came from

A podcast interview with **Nancy Baker Cahill** about her *4th Wall* app, and specifically her work placing augmented-reality pieces in contested sites in New Orleans. I am from New Orleans, so that landed harder than it otherwise might have — art sited where the place itself carries the meaning, reachable by anyone with a phone.

Her argument is that art has to invite interaction to have impact, and that smartphones democratise access to it. My disagreement was narrow and specific: **a phone limits how much agency it can actually hand over.** Holding a screen up to a wall is still looking. A headset lets the viewer stand inside the work and change it, so that is what I built.

*(Cahill turns up again two years later as one of the four precedents in my thesis. This is where I first read her.)*

## The design decision

**Failure subtracts instead of punishing.** Missing a block does not deduct points or end a run — it dims the particles and lowers the music. There is no scoreboard telling you how you did, because the state of the artwork *is* how you did. If it is quiet and dark, that is a description of your performance rather than a judgement of it.

That is the whole argument about co-creation, reduced to one rule. A score would have made the art a container for a game. Making the art itself the readout means a player cannot help authoring it.

The other decision worth naming: blocks spawn relative to the player's field of view rather than at fixed positions in the room, so a full-sphere game stays playable by someone who does not know to turn around. It keeps the piece honest about being 360° without requiring the audience to already understand that.

## The lineage

Guitar Hero 3, Rock Band, Rocksmith, and still Clone Hero — then Beat Saber, Pistol Whip and Synth Riders for what the genre becomes when the instrument is your body. The structure came straight from those; the difference is that in a rhythm game the visuals reward you, and here they *are* the point.

## Outcome

Built and published as a downloadable Quest 3 build on itch.io as the course's AR art project. My own conclusion at the time was that it did not fully meet my initial expectations, and I stand by that — it works, it makes its argument, and the particle system carries more of the piece than the interaction does.

## What I'd change

**Let the artwork persist.** Everything the player builds disappears when the session ends. A piece about the audience co-creating should keep what they made — export it, leave it in the room, let the next player arrive to find the last one's work already there. That single change would turn a rhythm game with an argument into the argument itself.

**Commit to the site.** Cahill's work means what it means because of where it is placed, and mine runs anywhere. Anchoring it to one real location — with the passthrough actually showing that location — is the version that inherits what I admired about *4th Wall* rather than only citing it.
