# Interview With Eternity

**A VR experience where you are a soul before birth, choosing whether to be born mortal — and the only thing you can do is look.**

*Solo · Immersive Media: XR History & Theory (XR 5010), Northeastern · 2024 · Unity, Quest 2 and 3, sideloaded build*

## The premise

You wake as a soul in an eternal void, before a life, and you are interviewed. The question underneath the questions is whether you want to be mortal. You answer, you consider what either choice costs, and you sit inside the weight of it.

## The decision the whole piece rests on

**You cannot do anything.** There are no hands, no controllers, no objects to pick up. You can look around the void and nothing else.

This is the opposite of how VR is usually built. Agency is the medium's headline feature, and removing it is normally a bug report. Here it is the mechanism: an immortal soul with no body and no power to change the course of time should not have a grab button. Constraining the player's physical capability to match the avatar's condition is what makes the helplessness legible rather than merely described — you are not told the void is inescapable, you find that there is nothing to do about it.

It also has a consequence I did not design for and would keep on purpose now: a gaze-only experience needs no controllers and no hand dexterity at all, which makes it one of the more accessible things I have built.

## Mirrors that show nothing

There are mirrors in the void and they have no reflection.

That came out of a specific research finding. **DeVeaux et al.** studied attribute discrepancy in social VR — how closely people's avatars resemble their physical selves in *Rec Room* and *VRChat* — and found differences between demographic groups in whether users built realistic or idealised versions of themselves. What interested me was the case the study could not cover: **what happens when a user is given no appearance to choose at all?**

So the player has no body to look at, and the mirrors refuse to supply one. The intent is that the physical self and the virtual self collapse into the same blank thing — a canvas rather than a character. Nobody has to negotiate a discrepancy between who they are and who they are represented as, because there is no representation.

The other reading came from **Blascovich and Bailenson's *Infinite Reality***, and specifically the idea that virtual reality is a condition of the mind rather than a place you visit. If that is true, then an experience about the mind does not need a world built out — it needs a void and a question.

## Where it came from

I grew up in a Christian household and was handed a place to put my faith. Getting older meant wanting to find my own, and that included making some peace with what happens after death. Thinking hard about the possibility of nothing still unsettles me, and what reliably helps is saying so to someone.

The phrase people reach for at the end of anything good is *why does it always have to end?* I wanted to build the other half of that question — **where does it all begin?** Where does a soul come from, does it decide anything, and do those decisions shape who it arrives as. The experience does not answer any of that. It just refuses to let you look away from it.

## What I'd change

**Capture it.** There is no video and there are no screenshots of this experience anywhere, which is why this page has no images. For a piece whose entire effect is atmospheric, that is the most damaging omission on the site — a still of a mirror with nothing in it would do more work than any paragraph here.

**Earn the silence.** Removing interaction is the right call and it puts all the weight on writing, pacing and sound. Gaze is the only input, which means gaze is the only thing I can design with, and I used it as a way to answer questions rather than as the instrument it could be — how long you hold something in view, what you refuse to look at, what happens when you look away.
